Leak 4 — The endgame errors are finishing errors, not technique gaps

Only 14 of the 50 games reached an endgame, and those 197 moves carry a 22.3% error rate — still your worst phase, but only just ahead of the middlegame's 21.0%. The interesting part is which positions the errors come from. Of the 44 endgame mistakes and blunders: 21 were played from an already-winning position (better than +3.00), 17 from an already-lost one, and only 6 from anything close to contested.

So this is not a hole in your king-and-pawn theory — barely a tenth of these are decisions that were still live. It is the same leak as everywhere else, arriving last: you reach a won endgame and stop finishing it. King moves are the specific carrier, at a 30.6% error rate across 62 of them.

Openings to consider

Ranked by what the data actually supports. Note that the first recommendation is to change nothing.

Keep
Stay with 1…e5 against 1.e4

It produces your two best-scoring structures. Your first ten moves are 93.7% clean and leave you at −0.19 on average. There is no repertoire-shaped problem here to solve.

Patch
Learn one line each against the early-d4 tries

Eleven games, 31.8%, a pawn down by move 12 — your worst 1.e4 cluster, and the recurring …exd4 mistake lives here (move 15 exd4 twice, −5.04 average). Three short lines cover it: Scotch 4…Bc5; Scotch Gambit 4.Bc4 Nf6 5.e5 d5; Center Game and Danish — take the pawn, then hand it back with an early …d5 and finish developing.

Patch

Nine games at 22.2% — your single worst cluster, and you met 2.Bc4 with 2…Nc6. Switching to 2…Nf6 transposes into the Two Knights structures you already reach eleven times a set, meets 3.d3 with …c6 and …d5, and meets the Vienna's 3.f4 with …d5. One idea closes both holes.

Replace
Pick one system against 1.d4 — the Slav

Twelve games at 41.7% and −1.02 by move 12, spread across Queen's Gambit Declined, Ragozin, Chigorin and two London System games with no repeating structure. 1…d5 2.c4 c6 gives you a single pawn skeleton against everything and solves the light-squared bishop before it becomes a problem. Learning one structure beats half-knowing four.

Only if

1…e5 2.Nf3 Nf6 removes the Italian, Ruy Lopez, Scotch and Four Knights from your preparation in a single move. But it also trades away the two structures you score best in, so this is worth it only if the sheer number of White systems is what's burning your clock — not as a fix for the leaks above.

Pattern 5 — The …Bb4 line is a bad bargain, not an open question

Follow the 4.c3 road down one level at a time and the damage localises:

The obvious read is that you have no answer to the check. You do: you play Nc3 in twelve of the thirteen, and it scores 41.7%. Your reply is settled and it is fine. What is wrong is the bargain — you are volunteering into a structure that costs you roughly twelve points against your own baseline, every time Black takes it.

You score 72.2% with the d3 setup against 3…Nf6 over 54 games, and 49.2% with 4.c3 against 3…Bc5 over 61. You have never tried transferring the first into the second. It is the same structure you already play well, and it costs no new theory.

Twenty of 37 were unsound, and sixteen of those twenty games were losses. Before playing it, say the next two forcing moves out loud. If you can't, the sacrifice is a mood, not a line.

Prep

Thirteen games reach it and they score 38.5%, with your settled 7.Nc3 managing 41.7%. Since the reply is not the problem, do not spend prep on it — playing 4.d3 avoids the position entirely. If you keep 4.c3, at least know the follow-up to 7.Nc3 through move 12 rather than improvising from move 8.

Habit

Error rate triples from 5.1% to 20.7% between moves 8 and 11. That is where book ends. Build one deliberate pause there instead of reaching for Re1 or a queen sortie.

The thread

One diagnosis, both colours

As Black you score 22–32% in the open and gambit clusters and 65–100% in the slow Italian and Ruy structures. As White the slow d3 Italian beats the sharp d4 Italian by fifteen points over 176 games. In both cases the mechanism is identical: sharp positions demand accurate calculation on every move, you make one loose heavy-piece move around move 20, and the material goes.

The repertoire changes above are worth a few percentage points. The two habits — checking what is hanging before every heavy-piece move, and knowing how to convert a one-pawn edge — are worth considerably more. The 0-from-8 record in games where you were clearly better but never winning is the number to fix first.

Method & caveats

  • All 875 games analysed with Stockfish at depth 22, single PV. Evaluations stored White-centric; Black-perspective figures negate them.
  • Average centipawn loss is capped at 1000 per move so forced-mate scores don't dominate the mean. Individual move costs quoted are uncapped.
  • Material-drop claims are not taken from the database's hanging_piece label, which flags any attacked, undefended piece after a move regardless of cause. They are re-derived by running static exchange evaluation on every legal capture in the resulting position.
  • The ingest pipeline forces classification = 'blunder' inside some of its checkmate-handling branches, which mislabels moves whose evaluation improved — 13 such moves on your side as Black, 12 of them the engine's own top choice, mostly the move that delivered mate. Every error count here additionally requires eval_delta < 0, so a move has to actually lose ground to be counted. This trims 262 Black errors to 246 and pulls the endgame error rate from 26.9% down to 22.3%, since mate scores cluster there.
  • Attribution was audited by replaying all 13,554 analysed moves in the scoped games against their PGNs: every stored move matches its ply, the stored side-to-move agrees with ply parity on all 3,226 Black-game plies, no game is analysed twice, and every counted move belongs to the colour you played. Your moves are even plies as Black (1,600 of 3,226) and odd plies as White (5,213 of 10,328).
  • Asking only "what is hanging after the move" makes the same mistake as that label: a piece can already be lost before you move, in which case the move did not drop it. So each figure quoted is the avoidable loss — the material available to the opponent after the move played, minus the least available after any other legal move. A move played while already in check, with the rook hanging either way, contributes nothing.
  • Per-opening samples in Part I run 6–12 games; score percentages at that size are noisy. The move-12 evaluation average is the steadier signal, and it agrees with the score direction in every cluster shown.
  • Part I covers the 50 most recent games as Black (13 Jun – 19 Aug 2026), 46 of them at 5+5. Part II covers all 180 Italian games as White since Aug 2025 — a longer window, chosen for sample size.
  • A daily job keeps ingesting new games, so the window is pinned to games played before 20 Aug 2026 rather than left as a rolling "last 50". Every figure here re-computes to the same value on a later run; re-cutting against fresher games is a one-line change.
  • Opening names come from the chess.com ECOUrl header; the stored opening_name column is unpopulated for recent games.
  • Every board is a real position: the FEN, the move played and the engine's move come from the database, and each was checked by replaying the game's PGN to that half-move before rendering. The two opening positions in the d3/c3 comparison are built by pushing the moves, and link to a real game that reached them.
  • The chess.com links open the game's analysis board at the move named in the link. Each game URL was confirmed to resolve; the half-move index follows chess.com's own numbering, so if a link lands a move early or late, the move named in the link text is the one to look at.
